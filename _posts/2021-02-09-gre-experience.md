# How impactful are my GRE scores for Graduate Admission process?
<br />
I recently came across this article written by Karthik Raghunathan from Stanford CS: [Demystifying the American Graduate Admission Process](https://nlp.stanford.edu/~rkarthik/DAGAP.pdf){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} &#9733;. This is the most cohesive source I have found until now, which elucidates every aspect of the admission process. I strongly recommend everyone to go through this. 

However, for those who are here lazy and just want to know how impactful their GRE scores are, here is what the above article reads: 
> The GRE General Test, conducted by ETS is a requirement for most of the top schools. However, the GRE 
Subject Test is usually optional for a Masters application. The GRE scores are mostly used as a negative filter for 
rejecting candidates. A poor GRE score can get the candidate rejected, but an exceptionally high GRE score will still 
not be enough (by itself) to guarantee him/her an admit. Candidates with full (or almost full) marks in the 
quantitative section are viewed favourably.
A great GRE score will help the candidate a lot, especially if he/she comes from a lesser known university that 
the committee has not heard of. In spite of a good academic track record in such a college, it might be tough to 
convince the committee if the GRE score is low, since it casts suspicions on the overall quality of the candidate's 
undergraduate college. A good GRE score, on the other hand, provides further evidence in support of the candidate 
and assures the committee that the high GPA is not merely a fluke due to studying in a rather uncompetitive college.
A consistently bad percentile in each of the three GRE sections definitely stacks the odds against the candidate. 
However, a slip up in one of the sections (especially if it is the Verbal or the AWA section) can be overlooked, 
provided the rest of his/her profile is genuinely good. 

For admission in Computer Science in top graduate school programs, it is safe to have: 
* Verbal: 153 or more
* Quants: 167 or more
* AWA: 4 or more

However, this is not a rule, just something which you might want to have to increase your chances of getting into top universities.

* A Verbal Reasoning score is reported on a 130–170 score scale, in 1-point increments.
* A Quantitative Reasoning score is reported on a 130–170 score scale, in 1-point increments.
* An Analytical Writing score is reported on a 0–6 score level, in half-point increments.

# My preparation timeline
<br />
I was able to score 328/340 with some smart work and a scintilla of dedication. I was irked after my first unsuccessful attempt and GRE seemed like a daunting task to me. However, I knew that I needed a decent score to fulfill my aspirations to pursue graduate studies abroad. It motivated me to study again and rectify the mistakes I made before. In the end, it turned out to be quite fruitful! I’ll retrospect here my journey from the day I officially started the preparation.
My GRE preparation timeline goes something like this:

* June 2020: Started learning words, spending an hour or so each day
* July 2020: Started practicing verbal and spend 2-3 hours every day
* Aug 2020: Was supposed to give GRE but had to postpone because I tested positive for Covid-19
* Sept 2020: First attempt
* Dec 2020: Started preparing again for the 2nd attempt 
* Jan 2021: Revised concepts, practiced sums, gave a few mocks 
* Feb 2021: D-day, GRE.

With proper planning, a score way better than mine, probably 330+/340, is easily achievable.

# Should one take coaching for GRE? 
<br />
Nothing against the people who do take coaching, but I strongly feel that there is no need for it. According to me, GRE tests your ability to apply knowledge rather than learn intricate things. Practice is all you need so unless you are someone who won't study until your coach pressures you to, GRE classes are not worth the money.

# How did I prepare for AWA? 
<br />
It comprises of 2 essays, of 30 minutes each. To be honest, the score of AWA does not matter drastically but it helps to have a 4+ (which is easy to get given that you are a decent writer). However, AWA is the first section of GRE, and performing well in this section helps you boost your confidence for the subsequent Quants and Verbal Sections. The writing sections are not testing knowledge but instead structure and critical thinking ability. If you have a flair for writing, this should not be too challenging. However, if not, then take up a few topics and write your essays.

I practiced 3 issue essays and 2 argument essays for appearing for the exam. While giving the mocks, I realized that I slightly struggled in coming up with ample points to support my issue essay arguments. Spending a lot of time in opening the issue essay aggravated the problem. Thus, to alleviate it, I went through the pool of Issue Essays (list of 150 essays) and noted the ones which perplexed me ([this](https://docs.google.com/document/d/19Pfu_IcPMPbxVfz5efyTLO5uxPnw5xHGiqd3GAkGDgE/edit?usp=sharing){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} is my list). I then surfed the net, found essays written by random people on various portals, and read them to gather a few points. I even prepared a dummy starting paragraph for my Issue essay which was loaded with GRE words to leave a good first impression and to save time for my body paragraphs. I found the argument essays quite straightforward so I did not pay much heed while preparing.

The start and end I prepared for my essays can be found - [here](assets/AWA.pdf){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}. It is needless to say that copying the text as it is will not help you since ETS has a provision for plagiarism in AWA essays. 

Does the GRE essay come from the pool of topics specified on the ETS website?
**Yes**, both the argument and essay topics come from the essay or argument pool as specified in the ETS site (links present at the end of this answer)
 
People often skip the AWA section while appearing for mock tests. Please don't do that. I know it is vapid and tedious, but it will help you judge yourself better. There are high chances of you making a silly mistake in the last Quants section if you wrote the essays and you are not used to writing tests with such long time formats. Adhere to writing both the essays diligently. It indeed will bear you sweet fruit.

For people targeting STEM courses, a perfect score (170/170) in quants should be the only target. It is fairly easy to achieve this if you manage to avoid silly mistakes and excel at time management.

Whenever I erred in any question during my mocks, I used to go back to that chapter, analyze what I missed and make a note of it for future reference. I feel that making a list of mistakes, is of paramount importance since it helped me ensure that I don't make the same silly mistake twice. 

I would personally suggest starting with Manhattan 5lb and Nova GRE Math Bible for someone who wants to learn concepts. Even though the sums in these books are straightforward, they do help in the revision of concepts and certain areas that are easy to miss (like box plots). You should not skip a single thing in quants, however trivial it might be. A few of my peers did not pay heed to topics like box plots, GRE math conventions, the sum of interior angles, etc, and were surprised to see multiple questions relating to it in their actual GRE. 

Lastly, if you are shooting for the moon? You might want to solve GMAT sums which are harder than GRE quants.

# How did I prepare for Verbal? 
<br />
Firstly, for verbal in particular, I strongly recommend solving the Official ETS Verbal Guide before your actual GRE, since it is important to get accustomed to ETS’ way of testing. 

During my 2nd attempt, I got a subscription to [GregMat Plus](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J0ywReE4qKo){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}&#9733;. Nothing has been more helpful. The affordability of GregMat is one of its high points. You will rarely find a course that is so well structured and so credible for such a low price in the world of test prep. Monthly payment of $5 gives you access to a magnitude of different learning material. 

The verbal score does not matter as much as the Quants score does. For people aiming for STEM courses, a score in the vicinity of 152–155 in verbal should be enough. However, a good verbal score helps to escalate the total GRE score. Majority of my preparation for verbal included solving [The GRE Big Book - 27 Old GRE tests](https://www.amazon.com/GRE-Practicing-Take-General-Test/dp/0446396001/ref=sr_1_6?keywords=gre+the+big+book&qid=1554738135&s=gateway&sr=8-6){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} &#9733;. Even though the format has changed, but the short and long passages are quite relevant. Solving the text completion and critical reasoning questions from this book is also not a bad idea. Apart of the Big Book, I solved [The Princeton Review - 1014 practice questions](https://www.amazon.in/Practice-Questions-Graduate-School-Preparation/dp/0307945383){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} and [Kaplan's Quiz](https://www.kaptest.com/gre){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}. I would suggest not to solve Manhattan 5lb for verbal since the questions are not similar to what ETS asks. They are more focused on vocab rather than logic.

Mastering reading comprehension is of paramount importance since half of the questions in verbal are passages. [Here](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7pUvqCcSlcU&list=PLFzybs-0YWa0PxFl_5oVK41xBfLxPkNN1){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} are some of the reading strategies which might be helpful. Sentence equivalence questions can't be relied upon since you get only 8/40 of them and you can never be sure of the vocab. However, the [Pairing Strategy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=szdk6IGF9hQ){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} works surprisingly well! You only get 4/40 critical reasoning questions, which are slightly more difficult than other questions partially because they have a 5-6 line passage for a single question. It's okay to not spend a lot of time preparing for such questions. I personally find text completion questions quite straight-forward most of the time. I highly recommend watching [these tricks](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=p9qAG0rXTG4&list=PLFzybs-0YWa2YUvcjq4sAqHd_SFd2XHtV){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} (specially the math strategy) which were very helpful.

# What did I do differently the 2nd time?
<br />

As I mentioned before, I took the GRE twice and scored 11 marks more in my 2nd attempt. Having talked to numerous seniors and random people on the internet, I realized that it is extremely difficult to increase your score if you retake the exam within a month. Speaking from my personal experience, I know at least 15 peers who retook the test but score the same (11/15 scored the same!), sometimes with a varied distribution between quants and verbal. Even though the data size is diminutive, it is safe to infer that taking some more time to practice voraciously before giving the exam again would be the right thing to do. 

I resolved most of the material again during my 2nd attempt. However, this time I solved everything entirely as opposed to the last time when I solved only a few sections, that too partially. It is important to practice all the sections and all types of questions. I solved  [The GRE Big Book - 27 Old GRE tests](https://www.amazon.com/GRE-Practicing-Take-General-Test/dp/0446396001/ref=sr_1_6?keywords=gre+the+big+book&qid=1554738135&s=gateway&sr=8-6){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} &#9733; properly this time - all 27 tests. I even practiced text completion and critical reasoning questions. I used to watch the video explanation of the solutions on GregMat Plus, which was very conducive in helping me understand the crux of the problem and the concepts with ETS tries to test. For quants, I solved the Kaplan Quizzes and rectified my mistakes in the mock tests. Speaking of vocab, I learned fewer words for the 2nd attempt. This was because after the 1st attempt I realized that ETS focuses more on your solving skills rather than your vocab. Most of the 3rd party study material do the complete opposite which is why I avoided using them as much as I could. 

Having said all that, I feel that GregMat Plus was one of the most important reasons for my improved verbal score. I highly recommend it to everyone. I used it to look for solutions to questions I solved incorrectly in The Big Book and watched the lecture series for text completion and reading comprehension sections. Apart from that, I practiced Vocab from his lists along with other important lists on his site like "Double Duty words", "most frequent words" etc. Even though I did not use it quite extensively, a $5 subscription is completely worth it!

One more piece of advice - try solving the mock tests seriously and emulate the test environment as much as possible. Do not skip the essay sections and experiment section. Basically, instead of drastically improving in a particular section, I tried doing everything slightly better. 

> Aggregation of marginal gains —"The whole principle came from an idea that if you break down everything you could think of that goes into riding a bike, and then improve it by 1%, you will get a significant increase when you put them all together." ~Brailsford
